Skills & referee learning path

Connect the target, your movement, the blade action, and the referee’s explanation. Start with one question. Move to the next stage when you can explain the idea in your own words.

This is a learning sequence, not an age test or a referee qualification. Read and observe independently; learn new blade techniques and partner drills with your coach and appropriate fencing equipment. The quick map

Target areas, lines, and space

A target names where a hit lands. A line describes an opening. A piste zone describes where the fencers are standing.

MapWhat it means
Head / mask · 头部／面罩The conductive mask is a target.
Arm · 手臂Upper arm and forearm; not the hand.
Torso · 躯干Front, sides, shoulders and back within the valid area.
Inside / outside · 内侧／外侧Describe openings relative to the fencer’s weapon side, not the viewer’s left or right.
High / low · 高线／低线Relative openings on the upper-body target; low does not make the legs valid.
Piste · 剑道Locate the start lines and rear warning area before comparing distance.

The lower target boundary follows the tops of the hip bones. Hands, legs and feet are excluded. There is no universal FIE “attack zones 1–5” scoring map; ask what numbered targets mean in your coach’s drills.

  • British Fencing: weapon targets
  • FIE: target, t.97–98

Guard is a position; parry is an action

Holding a guard does not prove that an attack was parried. A successful parry prevents the incoming blade action from arriving. Mere blade contact is not enough to identify the whole exchange.

Number and nameBroad protection / vocabulary
1 · Prime · 一位Inside / front of the torso. Confirm the saber form with your coach.
2 · Seconde · 二位Lower outside line, toward the sword-arm-side flank.
3 · Tierce · 三位Upper outside line, toward the sword-arm side.
4 · Quarte · 四位Upper inside line, toward the non-sword-arm side.
5 · Quinte · 五位Overhead protection for the head in saber.

These are broad line labels, not pose diagrams. Coverage overlaps: 1 and 4 can protect inside areas; 2 and 3 outside areas. The number describes a position, not a unique body zone or an automatic referee decision. Match the exact blade and hand placement to your coach’s saber teaching.

  • Sydney Sabre: numbered parries in its course

Movement and distance

Watch the feet and recovery before judging speed. A forward step alone does not establish an attack’s priority.

MovementRecognition cue
En garde · 实战姿势Ready to move and use the weapon.
Advance / retreat · 前进／后退Reduce or increase the gap.
Lunge · 弓步Reach forward, then recover balance.
Recovery · 恢复Return from the action to a usable ready position.
Step–lunge · 前进弓步Coordinate the approach and finish.

For saber, forward movement with the rear foot completely passing the front foot is prohibited. Review familiar, coach-approved footwork at home; new jumps or complex combinations belong in class.

  • FIE: saber movement, t.101.5

Name the blade action

The target tells you where. The action tells you what happened. Timing and the surrounding sequence matter.

ActionShort meaning
Cut / thrust · 劈／刺Hit with the blade or its point.
Direct / indirect attack · 直接／间接进攻Finish in the same line, or change around the blade.
Feint · 假动作Threaten an opening to invite a response.
Beat · 拍击Deflect the blade or draw a reaction.
Parry · 格挡Prevent the incoming attack with the weapon.
Riposte · 还击Offensive action following a successful parry.
Counterattack · 反攻An offensive action during the opponent’s attack.
Counter-riposte · 反还击Respond after parrying a riposte.
Remise · 连续进攻An immediate renewed action without withdrawing the arm.

A counterattack is not a riposte. A beat and a parry may both involve blade contact; identify their role in the sequence instead of relying on the sound alone.

Understand the referee

The scoring machine registers hits; the referee decides which touch can be awarded. Watch the sequence, then listen to the explanation.

What you hear or seeMeaning to learn
En garde / On guardTake the ready position.
Prêts? / Ready?Say clearly if you are not ready.
Allez / Fence / PlayBegin fencing.
Halte / HaltStop; do not start a new action.
Two lightsNot two points, and not proof of simultaneous attacks.
Referee / assistantsThe referee directs the bout and awards touches. Where appointed, judges or a video consultant assist with their assigned observations.
Yellow / red / black cardsWarning / penalty touch / exclusion. The offence determines the penalty; not every incident begins with yellow.

Learn the official illustrated signals for attack, parry, riposte, hit, point and annulment at t.63. Use the current rulebook; youth formats and equipment requirements also need the event organizer’s instructions.

Choose your learning stage

1 · Foundations: recognize and describe

Choose one module per session. Naming a clear observation is enough; you do not need to award every touch.

Targets, guards, and successful parries

Separate a body area from a defensive position and an action.

  • Use target names such as mask, arm, chest, and flank.
  • Compare the guard before the attack with the action during it.
  • Keep the coach’s numbered drill targets separate from parry numbers.

Try an observation

Pause a demonstration before contact. Name the position. Replay and describe whether an attack was actually stopped.

A fencer holds guard 5 before the opponent attacks. Has she already made a successful parry?

Reveal an explanation

No. You have identified a position. You still need to observe the incoming attack and the defensive action.

2 · Build your skills: follow the exchange

Connect actions into a sequence. Compare two reasonable options without assuming either always works.

Attack targets and choices

Notice the opening and how the attacker tries to reach it.

  • Look for a direct finish or a change of line.
  • Treat an apparent feint as a hypothesis until the continuation is visible.
  • Ask what the defender did in response.

Try an observation

Sketch an exchange with words: approach → apparent threat → response → finish. Mark guesses with a question mark.

A highlights clip shows a successful attack. Does that show how often the tactic succeeds?

Reveal an explanation

No. The edit selects moments. You need attempts and context beyond a selected success.

Defense, then the next action

Distinguish making an attack miss, stopping it with the blade, and counterattacking into it.

  • First describe whether the attack reached its target.
  • Look for effective blade defense before naming a riposte.
  • Follow the next action instead of stopping your observation at blade contact.

Try an observation

Compare the parry demonstration with one bout exchange. Use “guard → attack → defense → response” as your note headings.

A fencer hits during the opponent’s attack without first parrying. Should you call that a riposte?

Reveal an explanation

No. That fits counterattack vocabulary. Whether it scores is a separate priority question.

Three clear priority cases

Use simplified cases before judging difficult footage. Assume valid hits, functioning equipment, and no other infringements.

  • A correct simple attack beats a counterattack that fails to prevent the attack landing.
  • A successful parry followed by an immediate simple riposte has priority over the attacker’s immediate repeat hit.
  • Truly simultaneous attacks give no point when both hit. Two lights alone do not establish that case.

Try an observation

For one clear exchange, propose a call, name its decisive detail, then compare with the actual call if audible.

A attacks; B successfully parries and immediately makes a simple riposte. A’s immediate repeat hit also lights. With no other fault, whose action has priority?

Reveal an explanation

B’s riposte. The decisive event is the successful parry followed by the immediate response, not which light you noticed first.

3 · Challenge: test the explanation

Advanced learning means noticing exceptions and missing evidence, not making the fastest confident call.

Patterns, timing, and alternatives

Compare several exchanges before guessing a tactical plan.

  • Track changes in distance, rhythm, and chosen target.
  • Compare an attempt that worked with one that did not, if both are shown.
  • Explain an alternative and what new observation might change your mind.

Try an observation

Record three exchanges from a bout. Add one possible explanation and one competing explanation.

You saw the same target chosen twice. Does that prove the opponent always leaves it open?

Reveal an explanation

No. Keep the pattern provisional and look for a counterexample.

Point-in-line, preparation, and blade contact

These need the whole sequence and coach/referee discussion. Read t.15 and t.101–106 beside the example.

  • Point-in-line requires a straight sword arm and a point threatening valid target; when it was established matters.
  • If point-in-line is already established when the attack begins, the attacker must deflect the blade; mere contact is insufficient.
  • Attack on preparation means attacking while the other fencer is still preparing, before their attack has begun. Moving first alone is not the test.
  • A correctly executed attacking beat on the outer two-thirds of the opponent’s blade retains priority. A beat on the third nearest their guard gives them the right to an immediate riposte (t.104).
  • For uncertain contact or timing, describe the evidence before choosing between these cases.

Try an observation

Choose an unclear exchange. Write what evidence you would need: earlier frames, clearer blade contact, the finish, or the referee’s explanation.

Point-in-line was established before the attack. The attacker only brushes the blade without deflecting it. Is that contact enough to remove the line’s priority?

Reveal an explanation

No. Mere contact is insufficient under t.102.1. Still follow the full exchange before awarding a touch; a single image cannot show when the line was established.

Supporting your fencer

Your Role in Her Fencing Journey

You can be a useful fencing parent before you understand every call. Help your daughter feel welcome, arrive prepared, ask questions, and enjoy learning. Let her interests and her coach's guidance shape the next step.

Begin with her reasons

Ask what she likes: solving a tactical puzzle, being with teammates, learning a movement, or competing. Her answer may change. Make space for her goals alongside the family's practical limits.

  • What was enjoyable this week?
  • What would you like help with?
  • Is there something you want your coach to know?

Agree on your job

Offer reliable transport, practical help, and a calm welcome after class. Agree who checks equipment, reads event messages, and asks questions. Gradually hand over manageable tasks while staying available. Technical feedback should connect with the coach's plan.

Help her belong

Introduce her to teammates and help her find people she enjoys learning with. Show women fencers with different styles and let her choose whom she wants to watch. Avoid assumptions about how girls should move, compete, look, or express emotion. She can be ambitious, playful, quiet, or outspoken and still belong.

A small family agreement

Discuss these together and revisit them when school, interests, or competition plans change.

  • One thing she wants from fencing.
  • One kind of help she wants from you.
  • A realistic family budget and schedule.
  • How she will say that something feels uncomfortable or needs changing.

One useful next step: Ask your daughter what kind of help she would enjoy from you this week.

Understand the Saber Basics

Start with the playing space, four useful action names, and the difference between a registered hit and an awarded point. You can enjoy watching while still learning the more difficult decisions.

The weapon, target, and strip

Saber permits cuts and thrusts. The target includes the head, torso, and arms, excluding the hands; its lower boundary runs horizontally around the trunk at the tops of the hip bones. The strip, or piste, is the marked fencing area.

Four useful words

Name the actions before deciding who scores.

  • Attack: the initial offensive action.
  • Parry: a defensive blade action that prevents the attack arriving.
  • Riposte: the offensive action after a parry.
  • Counterattack: an offensive action into an opponent's attack.

Two lights are information

In saber, two lights do not mean two points. They also do not prove simultaneous attacks. The referee judges priority from the action sequence. A properly executed simple attack takes precedence over a counterattack that neither parries nor avoids it. A successful parry gives the right to an immediate riposte. Truly simultaneous attacks can produce no point. Even a lone light may be annulled for a rule issue.

Ask about the turning point

Try: 'Which action did the referee call the attack?' or 'Was there a parry before the response?' First movement, the loudest shout, and the first lamp you notice are unreliable shortcuts. Save uncertain exchanges for a coach or qualified referee; a poor camera view may hide decisive details.

Watch with a Useful Question

You do not need to narrate an entire bout. Choose one viewing task, watch a short sequence, and compare notes if your daughter wants to. Keep watching enjoyable rather than turning every clip into an exam.

A simple viewing loop

Begin at normal speed so you see the whole exchange. Replay only the detail that interests you.

  • Notice the space between the fencers.
  • Follow the approach, defense, and next action.
  • Listen to the official call after the exchange.
  • Describe what you saw before guessing the intention.

Listen to the referee in order

On guard / En garde: take the ready position. Ready / Prêts: say if not ready. Fence / Play / Allez: begin. Halt / Halte: stop; do not start a new action. Then listen to the whole explanation and look for the awarded point. A referee’s left and right may not match your camera view.

Choose a viewing lens

For movement, watch step size and balance. For tactics, look for a choice that repeats, then a change. For composure, notice what a fencer visibly does between touches. Avoid assigning feelings or personalities from a face, gesture, or single exchange.

Turn an impression into a question

Instead of 'She always rushes,' try 'I saw her finish from farther away in these two exchanges. What did the opponent do?' This gives your child and coach something specific to examine. One example is a clue; it does not establish a permanent habit.

Keep video in perspective

Check the weapon, event, date, and source. A senior international bout can inspire observation without providing a suitable home drill. Older footage may reflect different rules or interpretation. A match result page, replay search, and playable recording are different resources; check the label before expecting a video.

Three useful tactical questions

Did the fencer close the distance to finish, make the opponent’s attack fall short, or use a parry before responding? Then ask whether the choice changes in the next exchange. A retreat can be part of a deliberate plan; moving forward alone does not prove priority.

One useful next step: Choose one exchange and describe only what you can actually see.

What She Is Learning Next

Use this roadmap as a conversation with the coach, not a timetable or age test. A child may understand tactics well while still developing movement control, or need a familiar idea revisited in a new situation.

Foundations: recognize and explain

Look for growing familiarity with equipment routines, commands, target areas, basic movements, and the main action names. A useful sign is being able to explain one idea in her own words and ask for help when something is unclear.

  • Can she explain a familiar practice task?
  • Does she know whom to tell about an equipment concern?
  • Can she name one thing she wants to notice next?

Build Your Skills: connect choices

Work with the coach on how distance, timing, attack, and defense fit together. She may begin to compare two options, recognize why an action worked, and recover attention after a difficult touch. Notice the quality of her explanation and decisions alongside the score.

Challenge: adapt and reflect

A more experienced learner can test a plan, notice an opponent's response, and discuss an adjustment. She can also identify missing evidence in a clip. Challenging work need not mean harder physical work; it can mean a better question or a more careful explanation.

Use home learning lightly

Let her choose one lesson, a short clip, or a coach-approved task. Reflection and reading can be the whole activity. A video title such as '10-minute routine' is not a daily requirement. New techniques and partner blade work belong in coach-supervised practice with appropriate protective equipment; home movement should stay within the coach's agreed boundaries.

Keep a small record

Try three lines: 'I noticed…', 'My coach's cue is…', and 'My next question is…'. Use notes to remember useful discoveries. Avoid turning lesson completion, practice minutes, or quiz scores into a contest between siblings or teammates.

One useful next step: Ask your daughter and coach for one current learning goal.

Support Before and After a Bout

Agree on the kind of support your daughter wants. Some children welcome a short conversation; others prefer a familiar gesture and space. You can care about her goals while keeping your relationship larger than the result.

Before: simplify

Offer one practical check and let the coach's agreed cue remain clear.

  • 'Have you got what you need?'
  • 'What is your coach's one focus today?'
  • 'Would you like encouragement, help with your kit, or some space?'

During: be a steady presence

Follow the event's spectator and coaching rules. Stay in the permitted area and avoid giving competing technical instructions. Keep your reactions respectful toward both fencers and officials. Check beforehand what encouragement she finds helpful.

Immediately after: connect first

Make room for enjoyment or disappointment before analysis. Acknowledge what she says without insisting she feel differently.

  • 'I'm glad I got to watch you.'
  • 'That felt disappointing. I'm here.'
  • 'Would you like to talk now or later?'
  • 'What do you need before your next bout?'

Later: be specific and curious

When she is ready, ask what she noticed and what her coach wants her to try. Name a real action: 'You asked a clear question,' or 'You returned to your plan after that touch.' Avoid comparisons with another girl's body, temperament, ranking, or speed of progress.

When a call feels unfair

Try: 'I did not see it clearly. Let's hear the explanation and ask your coach about the correct process.' Help her use the event's clarification procedure calmly. A disputed call can be a question to review; it need not become the story of the entire day.

One useful next step: Agree on a phrase or gesture she would like from you before a bout.

Make Event Day Manageable

Treat the first events as learning experiences in a new setting. Confirm details with the organizer and coach: youth formats, entry categories, equipment requirements, scoring, and coaching arrangements can vary by event and federation.

Before you register

Choose an event that fits her interest, experience, and the family's resources. Confirm readiness with the coach and make the costs visible.

  • Eligibility, membership, entry category, and registration deadline.
  • Check-in closing time, venue, and travel plan.
  • Equipment requirements and any inspection arrangements.
  • Whether a coach will attend, available support, and fees.
  • Published bout format and expected schedule; allow for changes.

Understand the event map

Many events begin with pools: small groups fencing a set of bouts that help determine ranking. Direct elimination places fencers in a bracket where winners normally advance. Some events use different formats or additional bouts. Ask how this particular competition works rather than borrowing an Olympic format.

Pack from the coach's list

Check the actual required kit and suitable spares together. Label belongings and learn what she can check herself. Buy or replace specialist equipment with the coach's guidance.

  • Required protective clothing, mask, glove, and conductive equipment.
  • Weapons, body cords, mask cords, and agreed spares.
  • Registration information and useful contact details.
  • Water, familiar snacks, spare clothing, and a quiet activity for waiting.

At the venue

Locate check-in, equipment control if required, the assigned strip, toilets, and the meeting point. Follow official updates for bout order and strip changes. Give her manageable responsibility for being ready while keeping adult support available. Waiting is part of the day; leave room for it.

Before leaving

Confirm her competition is finished, collect kit, and check that the recorded result is correct through the event's process. Thank the people who helped. Let the trip home include ordinary family conversation; a full bout review can wait for an agreed time.

Build a Clear Partnership with the Coach

A useful partnership gives the child a voice, the coach room to teach, and the family clear expectations. Schedule substantive conversations outside the middle of a lesson or competition bout.

Ask for one shared focus

Request a specific current priority and an example of progress you can recognize. Include your daughter in the conversation where appropriate.

  • What is she working on now, and why?
  • What should we notice beyond wins and losses?
  • Which home activities are suitable, and which require supervision?
  • When should we review the plan together?

Coordinate group and private lessons

Ask how private work connects with group practice and which cue should carry across both. If she hears different language, bring an example and ask how the ideas fit. Keep a short shared note so home learning follows the current focus.

Discuss practical limits early

Be direct about travel, school commitments, costs, and her interest in competition. Ask which events or equipment purchases are useful now and what can wait. More lessons or more events should be a considered choice, not an automatic response to one difficult result.

Make questions and concerns welcome

Ask how the club handles parent communication, physical coaching contact, changing arrangements, photos, and safeguarding concerns. Ensure she knows a trusted adult she can approach. Listen when she reports exclusion, uncomfortable comments, or something that feels wrong; seek an appropriate response through the club's published contact, including an alternative to the coach involved.

Help her speak for herself

Rehearse a simple question: 'I understood the first step, but could you show me the next part again?' Offer to accompany her when useful. Growing independence should still include access to adult help.

Practise reading the referee’s decision

Choose an answer from each written example, then read the explanation. The complete described sequence matters.

Both lights come on

Think, then check

You can see two valid-target signals. The referee has not yet announced the phrase.

  1. Each fencer gets a point.
  2. Wait for the referee’s analysis of the actions.
  3. The one moving forward always scores.

Answer: Wait for the referee’s analysis of the actions.

Two lights tell you both signals registered. They do not establish priority or prove simultaneous attacks. Listen to the referee’s whole explanation and the awarded point.

Parry, then an immediate riposte

Think, then check

In this simplified example, Left makes a correctly executed attack. Right clearly parries, then immediately makes a simple riposte. Left renews the attack. Both hit; assume no other faults.

  1. Left scores because Left started first.
  2. Both get a point.
  3. Right’s immediate riposte has priority.

Answer: Right’s immediate riposte has priority.

The successful parry gives Right the right to riposte. In this described sequence, the immediate simple riposte takes priority over the renewal. Real footage must show the necessary actions clearly.

Only one valid-target signal

Think, then check

Only Left’s light appears. You have not heard the referee’s decision.

  1. Look to the referee to confirm the touch and any fault.
  2. The lights replace the referee.
  3. Right must have priority because Right has no light.

Answer: Look to the referee to confirm the touch and any fault.

A single valid-target signal is useful evidence. The referee still confirms the touch and considers faults or annulment. Wait for the call before announcing the score.

The camera misses the blade

Think, then check

You hear a sound, but the blade contact is hidden. Your daughter asks whether it was a parry.

  1. Every blade sound proves a parry.
  2. Say the view is unclear and ask the coach later.
  3. Whichever fencer celebrates first is correct.

Answer: Say the view is unclear and ask the coach later.

“I can’t see enough to tell” is a useful answer. A sound alone cannot establish the action. Save a question for the coach without turning the replay into an argument.

Words for useful support

Adapt these examples to your daughter and the support she wants.

Before a bout

“Would you like encouragement, quiet, or help with something practical?”

Let her choose your role. Keep tactical cues consistent with her coach.

Avoid adding a new technique, a medal target, or several instructions at the last moment.

After a loss

“I’m glad I’m here with you. Would you like to talk now or later?”

Give her room for disappointment before discussing what happened. Later, ask about one choice or discovery.

Avoid an immediate replay of every mistake or explaining how she should have won.

After a win

“What choice are you most pleased with? I enjoyed watching you work that out.”

Share her enjoyment and let her describe the moment in her own words.

Avoid making the next medal the first thing you discuss.

A disputed call

“I didn’t see enough to be sure. Let’s let you and your coach use the event’s process.”

Model calm disagreement and respect for officials. Ask privately about the rule later.

Avoid arguing from the stands or inviting your daughter to blame every result on the referee.

She feels she doesn’t belong

“You belong here as you are. What happened, and what would help you feel supported?”

Listen for a specific experience. Take exclusion or disrespect seriously and involve the appropriate club contact when needed.

Avoid “girls are just like that,” comparisons about appearance, or telling her to ignore mistreatment.

Competition-day checklist

Use this as a reading checklist or print it. Checkmarks and notes in the interactive guide need an active webpage.

Before the event

  • Check event details: Confirm the venue, arrival and check-in deadlines, format, equipment rules, and coach arrangements.
  • Check the kit together: Use the club’s list: complete required protective kit, working saber and connections, and any required spares. Ask the coach about problems; do not improvise repairs.
  • Plan practical needs: Pack familiar food, water, travel essentials, and anything the event requests. Leave time to arrive without rushing.
  • Ask about support: Agree where you will sit, what encouragement she wants, and who handles technical cues.

During the event

  • Let her own the task: Give her room to check her strip, report when called, and speak to her coach, with age-appropriate help.
  • Follow the event’s rules: Respect spectator areas, coach-access rules, officials, opponents, and restrictions on recording.
  • Watch one learning goal: Notice a practiced action, an adjustment, or a calm reset. You do not need to count every mistake.

After the event

  • Offer connection first: Let her choose quiet, a hug, practical help, or a conversation.
  • Choose one reflection: When she is ready: what did she notice, and what would she like to ask her coach?
  • Close the day: Collect belongings, acknowledge effort and learning, and make room for life beyond fencing.

Glossary

Advance 前进一步
A fencing step toward the opponent.
Allez / fence / play 开始
Common language forms of the command to begin.
Beat 击打剑身
A purposeful action against the opponent's blade.
Bout 一场比赛
A fencing contest between two fencers.
Cue 动作提示
A short reminder that helps you focus on one feature.
Direct attack 直接进攻
An attack that goes straight to its intended target line.
Direct elimination / DE 淘汰赛
A stage in which the winner advances in the bracket.
Distance 距离
The changing space between the fencers.
En garde / on guard 准备姿势
The command to take the ready position.
Evidence 依据
The detail that supports your explanation.
Feint 假动作威胁
A threat used to provoke a response before another action.
Fencing phrase 交锋过程
The connected sequence of actions in an exchange.
Foible / forte 剑身弱部 / 强部
Parts of the blade farther from / nearer to the guard.
Halt 停
The command that stops the fencing exchange.
Interpretation 解释或推测
Your explanation of what the actions might mean.
Lamé 金属衣
The conductive garment used for electrical scoring.
Mission 学习目标
One clear thing you want to notice or learn.
Observation 观察到的事实
Something visible or audible in the footage.
Parry 防守格挡
A defensive blade action that stops an offensive action arriving.
Parry 3 / tierce 第三防守姿势
A commonly taught saber parry for the outside line.
Parry 4 / quarte 第四防守姿势
A commonly taught saber parry for the inside line.
Parry 5 / quinte 第五防守姿势
A commonly taught saber parry protecting the head line.
Pattern 重复规律
An action or choice that appears more than once.
Piste / strip 剑道
The marked area where the bout takes place.
Point-in-line 剑尖对准
A straight sword arm with the point continually threatening valid target.
Pool 小组赛
A group in which fencers have preliminary bouts.
Priority / right of way 优先权
The conventions used to decide which action takes precedence.
Process goal 过程目标
A goal about an action or habit you can control.
Reflection 复盘
Thinking about what happened and what you learned.
Reset 调整状态
A short routine for returning attention to the next task.
Retreat 后退一步
A fencing step away from the opponent.
Riposte 还击
The offensive action after parrying.
Saber / sabre 佩剑
Two English spellings for this fencing weapon.
Salute 敬礼
The formal gesture of respect used in fencing.
Second intention 第二意图
An opening action used to draw a response for a planned next action.
Self-check 自我检查
A question used to see what you understand.
Tempo / rhythm 节奏
The pattern and timing of the actions.
Valid target 有效部位
The body area where a touch can count for this weapon.
